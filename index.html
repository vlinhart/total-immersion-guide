Total Immersion Freestyle

Total Immersion (TI) is a way of teaching freestyle developed by coach Terry Laughlin from 1989 onward. It treats swimming as a skill, not just fitness: reduce drag first, then add propulsion that comes from the whole body rather than from the arms. You swim long, balanced, quiet and relaxed, “like a fish, not a human”.

1

Balance

Stay horizontal without kicking hard. Release the head, lean on the chest, and the hips rise.

2

Streamline

Make the body a long, narrow vessel. Swim on your sides, extend the lead arm, and keep everything inside your shadow.

3

Propulsion

Hold your place in the water and let body rotation, timed with the spear and a 2-beat kick, move you past your hand.

Principles: why TI looks the way it does

Water is about 800 times denser than air. At swimming speeds, drag matters much more than power. A small cut in resistance saves more energy than a big increase in effort. Every TI technique comes from three ideas, in this order:

① Balance before propulsion

Your lungs float and your legs sink. Your body behaves like a seesaw pivoting around the chest. Most adults’ legs drop, so they kick hard just to stay level, which is exhausting and gets them nowhere. TI fixes this with position, not effort:

  • Release the head. Let the water support its weight, and look straight down.
  • Lean on your chest (“press your buoy”) to shift weight forward.
  • Keep a lead arm extended. It moves weight forward, like adding weight to the other end of the seesaw.

② Streamline: be a longer, narrower vessel

Long boats are faster than short boats. TI swimmers “swim tall”: the lead arm extends the waterline, and the body rotates so you slip through the water on your side, not plough through it on your chest.

  • Skate position: you are briefly on one side with the lead arm extended between strokes.
  • Front-quadrant timing: one hand is always out in front, so the vessel stays long.
  • Stay in your shadow: elbows, hands, knees and feet stay inside the outline your body cuts through the water.

③ Propulsion from the whole body

Instead of pulling water backward with the arm, TI teaches you to “hold your place” with hand and forearm and move your body past that point. The power comes from the rotation of the core, driven by the spearing arm and a light, well-timed kick.

  • Spear + kick + rotate happen together as one movement.
  • The arm is a connector between your core and the water, not the engine.
  • Quiet, effortless and repeatable. TI measures progress by fewer strokes per length at the same effort.

Anatomy of the stroke

Each part of the swim on its own, with an animated close-up. Pause any figure with its ❚❚ button, or open the moment in the Stroke Lab.

01

Head & spine: the weightless head

The head weighs about 4–5 kg on land. TI asks you to let the water hold it. Look straight down at the pool bottom, with the back of the head just at the surface and the crown pointing to the far wall. Imagine a laser from the crown, or a skewer through the spine: the head stays aligned with the spine and does not move independently except to breathe.

  • “Hide your head” between your arms
  • Crown leads, eyes down
  • Feel the hips rise when the head releases
02

Rotation: swim on your sides

The body rotates around the spine from one skate position to the other, about 30–45° each way. That’s enough for the recovering shoulder to clear the water, but not a full roll onto the side. Shoulders and hips rotate together, as one unit. The head stays still through the rotation.

  • Rotate from the hips, “like a skewer through you”
  • Rotation creates the power, the arm transmits it
  • Notice from above how the body narrows when rotated
03

The skate position

This is the home base of TI freestyle, and the position you briefly pass through after every stroke. The lead arm is extended on its railroad track, slightly below the surface. The other arm rests at the thigh. The body is rotated onto the lead-arm side, the face looks down, and the legs are relaxed and together.

  • Lead hand relaxed, fingertips slightly down
  • Long line from fingertips to toes
  • Stable, no wobble, no effort to stay up
04

Recovery: a relaxed swing

After the hand releases at the hip, the elbow leads the arm forward. The forearm and hand hang relaxed, close to the water. Because the body is rotated, the shoulder is already high, so the arm doesn’t need to swing wide. Recovery is the arm’s rest phase: no tension.

  • “Swing a relaxed, dead forearm”
  • Fingertips skim close to the water
  • Recover on your own side, never across
05

Entry & spear

The hand passes close to the head and enters about a forearm’s length in front of it, fingertips first, as if sliding into a mail slot or a sleeve. Then it spears forward and slightly downward to full extension, on a track in line with its own shoulder (railroad tracks, not a tightrope). The spear is driven by the hip rotating, not by reaching.

  • Enter near the head, extend underwater, not in the air
  • Spear to a target about 20–30 cm deep
  • Hands stay shoulder-width apart; never cross the midline
06

Timing: the patient lead hand

TI freestyle uses front-quadrant timing. The lead hand waits, extended, until the recovering hand has passed the head. Only then does it begin the catch. For a moment, both hands are in front of the head (watch the green box). This keeps the vessel long and gives the spear something to “switch” with.

  • “Patient lead hand”
  • Catch as the other hand reaches the mail slot
  • Think of it as a smooth switch, not a catch-up pause
07

The catch: hold your place

The catch starts the propulsive phase. The fingertips tip down and the elbow stays high, near the surface, while the forearm drops below it. Hand and forearm form one vertical paddle facing backward. Don’t press down on the water. That only lifts you. Instead, anchor the paddle and get ready to move the body past it.

  • High elbow, vertical forearm
  • Gentle, gradual pressure, not a grab
  • Hand stays under its own shoulder
08

Pull & push: move the body past the hand

With the paddle anchored, the body rotates and slides forward over it. In the water’s frame, the hand barely moves backward. It travels under the body, from under the shoulder to past the waist, and finishes at the thigh as the hip rotates out of the way. Then it releases, elbow first, into the recovery.

  • The body passes the hand, not the other way round
  • Power from hip rotation, not shoulder muscle
  • Finish at the thigh, without flicking water up
09

The 2-beat kick

TI uses a 2-beat kick: one small kick per arm stroke, two per full cycle. Each kick is a compact downward flick from the hip, timed with the opposite hand’s spear. As the right hand spears, the left foot flicks, and the diagonal connection drives the hip rotation. Between kicks, the legs are relaxed, close together, and inside the body’s shadow.

  • Right spear ↔ left foot, left spear ↔ right foot
  • Small kick: the foot stays inside the body’s shadow
  • The kick drives rotation, not forward thrust
10

Breathing: roll to air

Breathing is part of the rotation. As the arm on your breathing side pulls and recovers, the head turns with the body, around the spine’s axis, just far enough for the mouth to reach air. One goggle stays in the water. The head moving forward makes a bow wave, and the trough beside your head gives you air lower than the surface level. Return the face down before the hand spears. Between breaths, exhale steadily through the nose.

  • Turn, don’t lift: chin follows the shoulder
  • Keep the lead hand patient while you breathe
  • Learn to breathe on both sides (every 3 strokes, or alternate lengths)

Drill progression

TI builds the stroke in layers: balance first, then streamline, then timing and propulsion. Do each drill until it feels easy and stable before moving on. Fins can help at first. Breathe whenever you need to, by rolling to air or simply stopping and standing. Drill names and order vary slightly between TI books, DVDs and courses, but the goals are the same.

    How to practice

    TI calls it practice, not a workout. The aim is to imprint efficient movement, so every length should be done with attention.

    🎯 One focal point at a time

    Choose one thing to feel, like “weightless head” or “patient lead hand”, and swim short repeats (25 m) thinking only about that. Rest enough to stay relaxed. When it feels automatic, add the next focal point.

    🔢 Count strokes per length (SPL)

    Count every hand entry over one length. Fewer strokes at the same easy effort means a longer, more streamlined vessel. Find your personal range on easy lengths and work on staying in it as you add speed. Taller swimmers naturally take fewer strokes.

    ⏱ Tempo Trainer

    A small beeper under the cap sets your stroke rate. Find the tempo where your SPL feels comfortable, then make it slightly faster in small steps (e.g. 0.02 s per stroke) while keeping your stroke count. Speed = stroke length × stroke rate.

    ⛳ Swim Golf

    Add your stroke count to your time in seconds for one length (e.g. 18 strokes + 25 s = 43). Lower is better. It rewards both efficiency and speed, so you can’t “win” by thrashing or by over-gliding.

    Glossary

    Balance
    Lying horizontal without kicking to support the legs. You get there by releasing the head and leaning on the chest.
    Streamline
    Shaping the body to make the smallest possible “hole” in the water: long, narrow, everything inside your shadow.
    Skate position
    Rotated about 30–45° to one side, lead arm extended, other arm at the thigh. The pause point between strokes.
    Patient lead hand
    The extended arm waits until the recovering hand passes the head before starting the catch.
    Front-quadrant swimming
    Timing where one hand is always in front of the head, keeping the vessel long.
    Mail slot
    The entry point about a forearm’s length in front of the head where the hand slips into the water.
    Spear
    Extending the entering arm forward and slightly down to its target, driven by hip rotation.
    Railroad tracks
    Hands enter and extend on two parallel lines, shoulder-width apart, never crossing the midline (the “tightrope”).
    Catch / hold your place
    Setting a high-elbow forearm-and-hand paddle that anchors in the water, so the body can move past it.
    Switch
    The moment the lead arm catches and the recovering arm spears, as the body rotates from one skate to the other.
    2-beat kick
    One compact kick per arm stroke, with each foot flicking as the opposite hand spears.
    Bow wave trough
    The dip in the water beside the head, created by the head moving forward. It lets you breathe with your head low.
    SPL
    Strokes per length. TI’s main measure of efficiency.
    Kaizen
    Continuous, small improvement. TI’s attitude toward practice.
